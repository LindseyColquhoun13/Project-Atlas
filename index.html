Project Atlas

Tech Bootcamp — Phase 3 proposal · For review by Nik and engineering leads
What we're proposing — and what we need from you

This document proposes a new Phase 3 for the tech bootcamp, replacing the standalone accelerator setup and exercises with a single project simulation called Project Atlas. The technical work is the same. The framing is different: every task now lives inside a realistic client project so developers learn the stack, the AI workflows, and how Orium actually delivers — together, not in sequence.

We need your read on whether this feels authentic. Does the scenario reflect a real Orium project? Are the tickets written the way Orium actually writes them? Would a senior developer joining your team find this useful — or would it feel like a detour?

The full simulation is in Part 2. The ask of tech leads is in Part 3.

What success looks like

By Day 30, a developer should be able to:

  • Pull down a project and get it running locally
  • Understand the architecture and how data flows between systems
  • Navigate Jira and Confluence to find relevant context
  • Use AI effectively for orientation, planning, and implementation
  • Pick up a scoped story and deliver it with reasonable independence
  • Ask good questions before building, not after
  • Identify which system owns a piece of functionality, content, or data — and explain why

The goal of this onboarding is not to make you an expert in Orium's technology stack. The goal is to help you become productive on a client project as quickly as possible by understanding how Orium teams work, how our architecture is structured, and how to use AI to investigate, plan, and deliver work effectively.

By 60 days Contributing independently. Surfacing risks early rather than late. Starting to shape how work gets scoped and planned.

Welcome to Project Atlas

You're joining an active Orium project. A mid-market retailer is launching a composable storefront. Discovery is complete. The team is 4 weeks from launch. Your first story is waiting.

The point of Atlas is to practice contributing project velocity: understanding context quickly, using AI well, identifying ambiguity early, and moving work forward without waiting for perfect information.

Read the brief. Review the Jira board. Read the team comments. Then decide what you'd do first.

How Orium teams work

Orium projects are collaborative by default. PMs, designers, developers, and tech leads work together to move work forward. You're not expected to have every answer on day one. You are expected to identify ambiguity, ask thoughtful questions, communicate risk early, and bring recommendations — not just problems.

A note on scope

Project Atlas is intentionally representative, not exhaustive. Real projects may involve different CMS, PIM, OMS, payment, or integration patterns. The goal is to practice how to get oriented, understand ownership across systems, use AI to gather context, and ask the right questions before building — regardless of the specific stack.

When you're stuck

Getting unstuck is a core skill at Orium. Before escalating a problem:

  1. Use AI to understand the issue and identify possible paths forward
  2. Search project documentation, Jira tickets, and Confluence for relevant context
  3. Look for existing patterns in the codebase
  4. Clarify what you know, what you don't know, and what you've already tried
  5. Reach out to your buddy, project team, or the appropriate SME when needed
  6. If the issue creates project risk, communicate it early

The goal isn't to solve every problem alone. The goal is to move work forward by gathering context, reducing uncertainty, and involving the right people at the right time.

Project brief

How Orium developers use AI

Today, many Orium developers use Claude for reasoning, planning, debugging, and long-context work — orientation, understanding architecture, drafting tickets. For everyday coding and autocomplete, GitHub Copilot inside VS Code. For repo-wide refactoring or navigating unfamiliar codebases, Cursor.

The general principle: AI is most useful when the task has enough context to work with. For agents specifically — watch the output, keep tasks small, and validate everything. Token cost and time cost are real.

Before you escalate

When you hit a wall, the instinct to ask for help immediately is understandable — but on real projects, knowing who to ask and what kind of problem you have first is part of the job. Orium has SMEs across every platform area. Before you escalate, take a moment to categorize the issue:

Type of problem Who to reach
Accelerator pattern or architecture question Check the current SME list for Accelerator / Starter Kit
Contentful content model or API behaviour Check the current SME list for Contentful
Algolia search, indexing, or ranking Check the current SME list for Algolia
commercetools data, pricing, or API Check the current SME list for commercetools
Accessibility requirement or WCAG question Check the current SME list for Accessibility
Next.js or React architecture Check the current SME list for Next.js / React
Vercel deployment, edge config, or CI/CD Check the current SME list for Vercel

The SME list is maintained in the Best Practices Confluence space and changes as the team evolves — always check there for current contacts. If you're unsure how to categorize your problem, bring it to your buddy first. They'll help you route it.

Launch recommendation

The situation

It's day 8 of a 10-day sprint. Press releases are working. PDFs are partially scoped — compliance need is real, scope is still unclear. Filtering is not built. The Figma shows it. The client wants it. It's not in approved scope. Launch is in 10 days. The PM just asked you to give a recommendation on the call tomorrow.

Your options

Option A — Build everything before launch
Filtering, PDFs, full migration. Ship it all in 10 days.
Option B — Launch without filtering, resolve PDFs
Hit the compliance requirement. Defer filtering to a post-launch iteration.
Option C — Launch without filtering or PDFs
Scope is scope. Both items came in late. Document and defer both.
Option D — Recommend a short delay
Two unresolved items this close to launch is genuine risk. Push by one week.
Your deliverable

Before your buddy session: write up your recommendation. Don't just pick an option — state what you'd advise, what conditions must be true for that recommendation to hold, the risks you're accepting, and what you'd need from the client to move forward. Include any assumptions, dependencies, risks, or uncertainties that should be surfaced to the team or client. Orium consultants don't just choose A, B, C, or D. They say: we recommend B, but only if the client accepts X risk and signs off on Y.

What good looks like

By the end of Atlas, you should be able to join an active Orium project, navigate incomplete information, use AI to accelerate your work, and make a thoughtful recommendation under real constraints.

You're done when

  • You've used AI to summarize the project context and identify missing information before touching the codebase
  • You've identified the gaps and ambiguities across the Jira tickets and documented your assumptions
  • You've used AI to explore accelerator patterns before writing new code
  • You've completed the assigned implementation work for ATLAS-001 through 103, or documented what you'd split, defer, and why
  • You have a written recommendation for the launch decision with rationale and risks
  • Your buddy can push back on your thinking and you can defend it

How your buddy evaluates you

  • Not: Did they build the feature correctly?
  • But: Did they identify what was missing before building?
  • But: Did they use AI to move faster — not just to generate code?
  • But: Did they make reasonable assumptions and state them clearly?
  • But: Did they communicate risk rather than hide it?
  • But: Did they propose a path forward under real constraints?

Part 3 — What we're asking from tech leads

Your input before we move forward

For Nik and engineering leads · Please review before we finalize

What we're trying to solve

Our goal is to help developers contribute meaningful project velocity faster. Today, much of onboarding happens outside the context of a real project — the stack gets learned, the tools get configured, but the delivery model and decision-making expectations come later, on a live client engagement. Atlas introduces that context earlier, so developers learn the technology, AI workflows, and consulting behaviours together rather than in sequence.

What we need you to tell us

We've built this without real project data — which means the scenario, tickets, and team dynamics are plausible but not verified. Before we use this with actual new hires, we need engineering leads to pressure-test it:

  • Does the scenario feel like a real Orium project? Client type, architecture, constraints, timeline — does this ring true?
  • Are the tickets written the way Orium actually writes them? The gaps and ambiguity are intentional — but are they the right kind of gaps?
  • Is the tradeoff scenario realistic? Would a senior developer on your team actually face this kind of decision?
  • If a developer completed Atlas, what would they still be missing before joining your project? This is the most important question — we'd rather know now than find out on a client engagement.

What we're asking of tech leads ongoing

One conversation per new hire. 30–45 minutes.

At the end of Atlas, the developer presents their launch recommendation — their option, their rationale, the risks they'd accept. Your job is to push back on their thinking, not to give them the answer. This is the only part of Atlas that requires senior time. Everything else is self-directed.

Three questions to pressure-test this with us

  1. Does it teach the consulting behaviours we actually need — navigating ambiguity, communicating risk, recommending under constraints?
  2. Does it preserve the technical fundamentals developers need to contribute on real projects?
  3. Could we run this six months from now without heroic effort from tech leads?

If the answer to all three is yes, we'd like to move forward. If something feels off — the scenario, the ask, the structure — we want to know before this goes in front of a new hire. Your read on this matters more than ours.
